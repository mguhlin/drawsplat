A map of the evidence can help students explain a prediction about a character's next action while showing why the prediction remains uncertain.

## 1. Relevant details make a prediction more than a wish

"I think he'll leave." Why? A prediction gives readers a chance to connect what they know with what hasn't happened yet.

Choose a brief narrative and number its paragraphs. Use eight minutes to model the difference between an explicit action and an inference about what might follow. Partners get ten minutes to find two supporting details, explain the connection, and consider another reasonable possibility. Try "These details suggest ___ because ___." Ask what a reader would still need to know before being certain.

*For planning: Grade 4 ELA/Reading, 40 minutes; [Texas TEKS §110.6(b)(6)(F) and (7)(C)](https://tea.texas.gov/laws-and-rules/sboe-rules-tac/sboe-tac-currently-effect/ch110a.pdf). [TCEA Essential Learning Expectations](https://mglearn.github.io/eles): S3.2 and S4.2.*

## 2. Labeled links make the reasoning available to another reader

Give students fifteen minutes in [SplatWorks ConceptSplat](https://splatworks.org/solutions/concept-map/). Add nodes for the details, the prediction, and a competing possibility. Label connections so each reads as a sentence, and add source notes for the text evidence.

Ask a partner to read a link aloud. Does it explain why the detail supports the prediction? Choose a vague label to improve, then save editable JSON and export PNG. Students can begin with paper nodes, vocabulary cards, and spoken rehearsal. One projected map is enough to demonstrate the process.

## 3. A new paragraph checks whether students can build a second inference

Use seven minutes for an individual inference about a character's feeling, supported by two details from another paragraph.

The exact feeling may vary. I'd look for a connection the reader can defend and a willingness to stop where the evidence stops. That's a useful habit well beyond this particular story.
