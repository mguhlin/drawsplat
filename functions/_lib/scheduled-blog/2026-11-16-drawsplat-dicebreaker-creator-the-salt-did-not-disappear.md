Get students talking about a spilled-solution data set so they can explain why a lower mass reading does not, by itself, mean matter vanished.

## 1. A spill makes the system boundary worth discussing

The numbers went down. Does that settle it? Only if we know what was on the balance both times.

During eight minutes of modeling, weigh a covered container, water, and salt together before and after dissolving. Keep every part in the measured system and follow classroom handling procedures, including no tasting. Then give partners ten minutes with teacher-provided data showing a spill. Ask what left the measured system and why appearance alone can't explain the difference. Include evaporation and balance precision among the measurement issues worth considering. Dissolving itself isn't a chemical reaction.

*For planning: Grade 5 Science, 40 minutes; [Texas TEKS §112.7(b)(6)(C)](https://tea.texas.gov/laws-and-rules/sboe-rules-tac/sboe-tac-currently-effect/ch112a.pdf). [TCEA Essential Learning Expectations](https://mglearn.github.io/eles): S3.2 and S4.2.*

## 2. Required prompts keep the dice from choosing who has to think

Spend fifteen minutes with [SplatWorks Dicebreaker Creator](https://splatworks.org/solutions/dicebreakers/). Replace the six faces with prompts to name an idea, cite evidence, explain a relationship, find a misconception, change an example, and ask a useful question. Export a grid or strip, or share the student view.

Here's the part I'd insist on: both partners answer the evidence and explanation prompts, whatever they roll. Record the answers on paper. Let each student choose one answer to clarify after hearing their partner. Vocabulary cards and oral rehearsal help keep the discussion moving; a printed die works when devices are limited.

## 3. Another data set checks whether the explanation holds up

Use the last seven minutes for an individual response to new before-and-after measurements. Ask whether they support conservation of matter and which measurement limitation matters.

Listen for "what was measured" as well as "what the number says." If those are still getting mixed together, that's your next demonstration.
