A room of connected evidence panels can help students compare agricultural water supply with habitat changes before recommending a position on a fictional dam project.

## 1. Two consequences give the recommendation something to account for

Farmers need water. Habitats can change when a river is dammed. How do students explain both before deciding what they think?

Prepare a fictional Texas reservoir case with a before-and-after map, a water-supply need, and three stakeholder statements. Mark it as fictional. Spend eight minutes modeling a benefit and cost, identifying the affected people or habitats and supporting details. Partners get ten minutes to compare agricultural water supply and habitat change. Ask what additional information they would request. A fair explanation matters more here than reaching one prescribed policy position.

*For planning: Grade 4 Social Studies, 40 minutes; [Texas TEKS §113.15(c)(8)(A–C)](https://tea.texas.gov/laws-and-rules/sboe-rules-tac/sboe-tac-currently-effect/ch113a.pdf). [TCEA Essential Learning Expectations](https://mglearn.github.io/eles): S3.2 and S4.2.*

## 2. Connected panels let partners examine how the evidence fits together

Give pairs fifteen minutes with [SplatWorks SketchSplat VR](https://splatworks.org/solutions/sketchspace-VR/) and its printable room template. Plan a question, two evidence details, a relationship, a counterexample, and a conclusion. Photograph and crop the panels, then preview in full screen or split view. Glasses are optional.

Ask a partner to explain the connection between two panels before hearing the creator's account. Choose one unclear panel to revise and download the face images. Students can rehearse aloud or use vocabulary cards. A paper room with one projected preview offers the same chance to discuss the consequences.

## 3. Another case checks whether students can compare before deciding

Use seven minutes for each student to explain the purpose, one benefit, and one cost of a new road-clearing case, using details to support the response.

If the recommendation arrives before the comparison, ask the student to explain a consequence on each side. There may be a good idea there that needs a little more consideration.
