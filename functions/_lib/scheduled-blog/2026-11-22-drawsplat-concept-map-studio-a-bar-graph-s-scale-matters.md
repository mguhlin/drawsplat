Have students map the relationship between a bar graph and its data so they can explain why changing the scale doesn't change the votes.

## 1. Two displays of the same votes make the scale worth examining

Let's use fictional pet-picture votes: 4, 14, 8, and 6. Fourteen votes don't become seven votes because the graph uses intervals of two. That sounds obvious until you have to explain the bar!

In eight minutes, model a frequency table and a labeled graph with intervals of two. Give partners ten minutes to verify each category, answer a difference question, and find a combined total. Compare an intervals-of-one display. Ask students what changed and what stayed the same. Keep the figures clearly labeled as practice data.

*For planning: Grade 3 Math, 40 minutes; [Texas TEKS §111.5(b)(8)(A–B)](https://tea.texas.gov/laws-and-rules/sboe-rules-tac/sboe-tac-currently-effect/ch111a.pdf). [TCEA Essential Learning Expectations](https://mglearn.github.io/eles): S3.2 and S4.2.*

## 2. Labeled connections make students put the relationship into words

Spend fifteen minutes in [SplatWorks ConceptSplat](https://splatworks.org/solutions/concept-map/). Add nodes for the table, categories, frequencies, scale, and bar heights. Connect them with labels a partner can read as sentences. Include a note showing where the practice data came from.

A line that merely says "goes with" needs another look. Ask partners to choose one connection to improve and explain the revision. Save editable JSON and export a PNG. Students can use vocabulary cards and speak the sentences before typing. A paper map works nicely beside one projected example.

## 3. A fresh graph checks whether the explanation can be used

Use the last seven minutes for an individual reading of a new scaled graph, with one difference and one combined-total question.

Ask how the scale supplied the numbers. If the map looked convincing but the new axis caused trouble, you have a clear starting point for a follow-up. Bring the explanation back to one bar and its value.
