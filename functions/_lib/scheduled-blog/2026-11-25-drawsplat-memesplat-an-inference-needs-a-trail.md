Ask students to explain a character's feeling through a caption supported by two connected text details.

## 1. Two details give a feeling more support than a guess

"She's sad." Maybe so. What in the story led you there? A feeling word gets much more interesting when a reader can explain it.

Choose a brief narrative about a disappointment and number the paragraphs. In eight minutes, model the difference between an explicit statement and an inference, connecting two details to the character's feeling. Give partners ten minutes to mark their own evidence and discuss another possible interpretation. Try the stem, "These details suggest ___ because ___." Keep the explanation within what the text supports.

*For planning: Grade 4 ELA/Reading, 40 minutes; [Texas TEKS §110.6(b)(6)(F) and (7)(C)](https://tea.texas.gov/laws-and-rules/sboe-rules-tac/sboe-tac-currently-effect/ch110a.pdf). [TCEA Essential Learning Expectations](https://mglearn.github.io/eles): S3.2 and S4.2.*

## 2. A caption asks students to choose the meaning they can defend

Allow fifteen minutes with [SplatWorks MemeSplat](https://splatworks.org/solutions/memesplat/). Use a suitable template or local image and write a caption about the character's feeling. Add two sentences identifying the text evidence behind it.

Ask a partner whether the visual exaggerates what the story tells us. Choose one overstatement or unclear phrase to revise before downloading the PNG. Humor can help, but students still need to account for those two details. Let them rehearse aloud, use vocabulary cards, or draft on paper if access to a device is limited.

## 3. A new paragraph shows whether the reader can build another inference

For seven minutes, each student infers a character's feeling from a different paragraph and connects two relevant details.

The answer doesn't have to match a favorite emotion word of mine. I want to be able to follow the reasoning. If the details don't support the conclusion, compare them together and ask what else they might suggest.
