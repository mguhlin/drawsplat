Have students write about recreation and relocation in a fictional reservoir case so they can explain why people might judge the same environmental change differently.

## 1. Two perspectives give the comparison a human purpose

A place for boating can also be the site of someone's former home. How do students explain both experiences fairly? A shared case gives them somewhere to begin.

Prepare a fictional Texas reservoir scenario with a before-and-after map, a water-supply need, and three stakeholder statements. Label it as fictional. Spend eight minutes modeling a benefit and a cost, identifying who experiences each. Partners get ten minutes to compare recreation and relocation using details from the case. Ask what else they would want to know before making a recommendation. They don't have to reach the same policy position.

*For planning: Grade 4 Social Studies, 40 minutes; [Texas TEKS §113.15(c)(8)(A–C)](https://tea.texas.gov/laws-and-rules/sboe-rules-tac/sboe-tac-currently-effect/ch113a.pdf). [TCEA Essential Learning Expectations](https://mglearn.github.io/eles): S3.2 and S4.2.*

## 2. A short document makes room for evidence beside each perspective

Allow fifteen minutes with [SplatWorks WriteSplat](https://splatworks.org/splatworks/writesplat/) and a claim, details, and explanation scaffold. Students draft from their investigation notes, explaining the consequence for each stakeholder.

Ask a partner to find the source detail behind each judgment. Choose one sentence that needs a clearer connection and revise it before saving the document. A spoken rehearsal can help students represent an unfamiliar point of view. Offer vocabulary cards or a paper response when needed; one projected document can model the process for the class.

## 3. A different case checks whether the comparison habit carries over

Use seven minutes for each student to explain the purpose, one benefit, and one cost of clearing land for a road, with support from a new case.

If the answer gives only benefits or only costs, ask who else appears in the scenario. There's often another useful question waiting in a detail the reader hasn't considered yet.
