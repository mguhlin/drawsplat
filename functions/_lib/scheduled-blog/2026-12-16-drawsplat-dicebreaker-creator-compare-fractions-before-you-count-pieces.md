A partner discussion about two-fourths and two-eighths can help students explain why the same number of pieces does not always mean the same amount.

## 1. A shared numerator turns attention toward the size of the parts

Two pieces here, two pieces there. Are we done comparing? Only after we know what size those pieces are.

Use equal-length fraction strips divided into fourths and eighths. Spend eight minutes aligning the wholes and comparing one-fourth with one-eighth. Partners get ten minutes to model two-fourths versus two-eighths, then three-eighths versus five-eighths. Ask why the first comparison depends on part size and the second on the number of equal parts selected. Build before choosing symbols, keeping the wholes equal.

*For planning: Grade 3 Math, 40 minutes; [Texas TEKS §111.5(b)(3)(H)](https://tea.texas.gov/laws-and-rules/sboe-rules-tac/sboe-tac-currently-effect/ch111a.pdf). [TCEA Essential Learning Expectations](https://mglearn.github.io/eles): S3.2 and S4.2.*

## 2. Required explanation prompts give both partners a turn at the reasoning

Set aside fifteen minutes with [SplatWorks Dicebreaker Creator](https://splatworks.org/solutions/dicebreakers/). Replace the six faces with prompts to name an idea, cite evidence, explain a relationship, find a misconception, change an example, and ask a question. Export a grid or strip, or share the student view.

Whatever the roll, both partners must answer the evidence and explanation prompts. Record their reasoning on paper. Ask each student to choose one answer to clarify using the strips. Oral rehearsal and vocabulary cards can help. The printable version makes the conversation possible without a device for every pair.

## 3. Another comparison shows whether students can choose the right explanation

Use seven minutes for an individual comparison of new fractions with a common numerator or denominator, justified by an equal-whole model.

If the student says the numerators match and stops there, point back to the parts. What can they tell you about their size? That's where the comparison gets its meaning.
