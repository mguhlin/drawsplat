Give students connected evidence panels to explain how light interacts with different materials, comparing observations of reflection and absorption.

## 1. Contrasting materials give the vocabulary an observable difference

What happens when light meets this surface? What about that one? Students need something to compare before the labels "reflection" and "absorption" become useful.

Spend eight minutes modeling a ray diagram and its arrows. Prepare teacher-guided observations with a flashlight and card openings, a mirror, a pencil in water, and contrasting light and dark materials. Keep beams away from eyes. During ten minutes of partner investigation, connect observations to straight-line travel, reflection, refraction, and absorption. Focus the room on the contrasting materials. Keep claims within what students actually observed; don't invent absorption percentages.

*For planning: Grade 5 Science, 40 minutes; [Texas TEKS §112.7(b)(8)(C)](https://tea.texas.gov/laws-and-rules/sboe-rules-tac/sboe-tac-currently-effect/ch112a.pdf). [TCEA Essential Learning Expectations](https://mglearn.github.io/eles): S3.2 and S4.2.*

## 2. Connected panels give partners a comparison they can read

Allow fifteen minutes with [SplatWorks SketchSplat VR](https://splatworks.org/solutions/sketchspace-VR/) and its printable room template. Plan panels for the question, two observations, a relationship, a counterexample, and a conclusion. Photograph the drawing, crop the panels, and preview in full screen or split view. Glasses aren't required.

Have a partner explain how two panels connect. Choose one unclear panel to revise and download the face images. A paper room and projected preview are enough when devices are limited. Let students talk through the explanation and use vocabulary cards before committing it to the panels.

## 3. Another light interaction checks whether the comparison helped

Reserve seven minutes for each student to explain the light path in a new mirror or water-glass diagram and identify the phenomenon.

If the student only names the object, ask what the light does at it. That question brings the response back to the science the room was meant to explain.
