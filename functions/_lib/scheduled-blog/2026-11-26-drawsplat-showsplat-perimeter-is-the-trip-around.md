Let students explain perimeter as a trip around a boundary, with every side included in the calculation.

## 1. Tracing the edge makes the measurement clear

Before reaching for a formula, let's take a trip around the garden. Which lengths will we pass? Which squares inside the garden won't help answer that question?

Use grid paper and a rectangular garden drawing. Spend eight minutes modeling side labels, boundary tracing, and linear units. Give partners ten minutes with a six-by-four rectangle. They should explain why 6 + 4 + 6 + 4 gives 20 units. Follow with a polygon whose perimeter is 22 units and known sides are 5, 4, and 6. The missing 7 units must fit the complete boundary sum.

*For planning: Grade 3 Math, 40 minutes; [Texas TEKS §111.5(b)(7)(B)](https://tea.texas.gov/laws-and-rules/sboe-rules-tac/sboe-tac-currently-effect/ch111a.pdf). [TCEA Essential Learning Expectations](https://mglearn.github.io/eles): S3.2 and S4.2.*

## 2. Three slides let another pair follow the trip

Set aside fifteen minutes in [SplatWorks ShowSplat](https://splatworks.org/splatworks/showsplat/) for the question, a labeled model, and the explanation. Keep each slide readable. Speaker notes give students somewhere to rehearse why every side appears in the equation.

Have another pair identify a boundary segment and find it in the calculation. Choose one confusing label to improve, then save or export a PDF. Students can sketch the slides on paper if needed. Oral rehearsal and a small vocabulary card for perimeter, side, and unit can help a reader explain a correct model.

## 3. A missing side checks whether the whole boundary still matters

For the last seven minutes, each student solves a new missing-side problem with a labeled boundary and a checking equation.

If a side has disappeared from the sum, trace the trip again. That gives you a useful teaching move before asking the student to memorize another formula.
