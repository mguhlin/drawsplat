Ask students to explain why five-eighths is greater than three-eighths using a written response and a model with equal-size wholes.

## 1. Equal parts give the numerators something meaningful to count

Five pieces sounds like more than three. Here it is, because the pieces are the same size and the wholes match. Those conditions are worth saying aloud.

Use equal-length strips partitioned into fourths and eighths. Spend eight minutes aligning wholes and comparing one-fourth with one-eighth. Give partners ten minutes to model three-eighths and five-eighths, then two-fourths and two-eighths. Ask which comparison depends on how many parts were selected and which depends on part size. Have students build the models before choosing comparison symbols.

*For planning: Grade 3 Math, 40 minutes; [Texas TEKS §111.5(b)(3)(H)](https://tea.texas.gov/laws-and-rules/sboe-rules-tac/sboe-tac-currently-effect/ch111a.pdf). [TCEA Essential Learning Expectations](https://mglearn.github.io/eles): S3.2 and S4.2.*

## 2. A short written explanation connects the model to the symbol

Allow fifteen minutes in [SplatWorks MarkdownSplat](https://splatworks.org/solutions/markdown-studio/) under Question, Evidence, and Explanation headings. Start from a heading scaffold if needed. Describe or include the equal-whole model, then explain why the symbol matches it.

Ask a partner to find the sentence about the shared denominator. Choose one gap to clarify and check the rendered preview before exporting Markdown, HTML, or DOCX. Students can talk through the strips first, use vocabulary cards, or write on paper when one device is serving the class.

## 3. A changed comparison checks whether the explanation has some reach

For the last seven minutes, each student compares two new fractions with a shared numerator or denominator and justifies the answer using equal-size wholes.

If the response relies on "the bigger number wins," return to the strips. Ask what the number is counting. The model gives students a way to repair the reasoning instead of memorizing another rule.
