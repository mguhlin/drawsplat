Let students test an inference about a character's treasured object by writing quiz answers that explain the story evidence.

## 1. An unexplained action gives readers something worth inferring

The character keeps an old object. We know the action, but what might the object mean? That's where a reader has some thinking to do.

Choose a short narrative and number its paragraphs. Spend eight minutes modeling how two details can support an inference without turning it into a stated fact. Give partners ten minutes to mark evidence and consider a competing explanation. "These details suggest ___ because ___" is a useful beginning. Ask what the text leaves uncertain as well as what it supports.

*For planning: Grade 4 ELA/Reading, 40 minutes; [Texas TEKS §110.6(b)(6)(F) and (7)(C)](https://tea.texas.gov/laws-and-rules/sboe-rules-tac/sboe-tac-currently-effect/ch110a.pdf). [TCEA Essential Learning Expectations](https://mglearn.github.io/eles): S3.2 and S4.2.*

## 2. A reasoned answer key makes the quiz more than a guessing game

Allow fifteen minutes in [SplatWorks QuizSplat](https://splatworks.org/solutions/quiz-flashcard-studio/) for three items: an explicit detail, a misconception about the object, and an inference from a new example. Write the answer key with reasons on paper first.

Try the questions with another pair or use printable flashcards. Ask players to explain their choices, then choose one item or explanation to improve. Save or export the set. Students can discuss the reasons before writing, and vocabulary cards can help them separate "the author says" from "I infer." A projected quiz is enough for a class with limited devices.

## 3. A new paragraph checks whether the inference skill travels

For seven minutes, each student infers a character's feeling from another paragraph and connects two details to the answer.

I'd listen for the link between the details, not just two copied quotations. If the connection isn't clear yet, put the evidence side by side and ask what it suggests together.
