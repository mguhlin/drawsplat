A shared set of sources can help students compare a rail hub's effects on jobs and on moving goods.

## 1. Two kinds of change keep the history from becoming a single success story

A busy rail hub might change the movement of goods and the work available nearby. Those are connected ideas, but each needs support.

Bring two dated Texas rail maps and short, reliable accounts of a community and an industry. In eight minutes, model connecting a route observation to a change described in an account. Give partners ten minutes to compare an employment effect with an effect on transporting goods. Ask whose experience each claim describes and what the source establishes. A rail line alone doesn't tell us that every resident benefited.

*For planning: Grade 4 Social Studies, 40 minutes; [Texas TEKS §113.15(c)(4)(C)](https://tea.texas.gov/laws-and-rules/sboe-rules-tac/sboe-tac-currently-effect/ch113a.pdf). [TCEA Essential Learning Expectations](https://mglearn.github.io/eles): S3.2 and S4.2.*

## 2. A shared resource gives partners evidence they can discuss together

Allow fifteen minutes in [SplatWorks QRSplat Studio](https://splatworks.org/solutions/qrsplat/) to make a code for a teacher-selected public source approved for classroom use. Download the PNG, place it on a station card, and print the complete link beside it. Test the code on a student device.

Students visit the resource and record the two effects and their supporting details on paper. Ask partners to choose one connection to clarify. Project the source if devices are limited, and offer vocabulary cards or oral rehearsal as needed. The QR code helps with access; the explanation does the history work.

## 3. A new account shows whether students can support a fresh claim

Use the last seven minutes for an individual explanation of one railroad effect in another community, citing a particular account detail.

If a student swaps in an assumed benefit, ask what information would help establish it. That's a useful question to carry into the next source search.
