Help students build a respectful civic request by marking the details that explain why clearer library accessibility information would help.

## 1. A specific information gap gives the request a purpose

Imagine looking up a public library and being unable to find the accessibility information you need. What could you ask for, and who might be able to help?

Prepare a fictional library information sheet and a description of the appropriate office. Use eight minutes to model a request that names the problem, offers evidence, and asks for a feasible action. Give partners ten minutes to plan their own wording and identify the community benefit. Keep this a classroom exercise. Nobody needs to send the request, and students shouldn't assume they can change public information themselves.

*For planning: Grade 4 Social Studies, 40 minutes; [Texas TEKS §113.15(c)(15)(B)](https://tea.texas.gov/laws-and-rules/sboe-rules-tac/sboe-tac-currently-effect/ch113a.pdf). [TCEA Essential Learning Expectations](https://mglearn.github.io/eles): S3.2 and S4.2.*

## 2. Selective annotations connect the request to the source

Give pairs fifteen minutes with [SplatWorks PDFSplat](https://splatworks.org/solutions/pdfsplat/) and a teacher-provided PDF of the fictional information. Highlight the detail behind the request, then add an explanation of why it matters. Highlighting the whole page won't help the reader find it!

Ask a partner whether the annotation explains a useful connection. Let the writer choose one vague note to improve, then export the annotated PDF and keep the original. Students can rehearse their request aloud or use vocabulary cards before writing. Printed copies work just as well for the discussion when device access is limited.

## 3. A fresh problem checks the civic idea beyond the library

Use seven minutes for an individual response to a different fictional community problem. Ask for a voluntary action, the person or office who could help, and a supporting detail.

Look for a request someone could reasonably act on. If the response is simply "fix it," work together on identifying the specific help being requested. That's a useful writing lesson as well as a civic one.
