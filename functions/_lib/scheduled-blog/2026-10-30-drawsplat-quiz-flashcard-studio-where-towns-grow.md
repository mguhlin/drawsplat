Use QuizSplat in a 40-minute fourth-grade social studies lesson to help students build, test, and independently apply evidence-based explanations of why towns grow where they do.

1. Comparing maps gives students evidence for their explanations

A river on a map is easy to spot. Explaining why a town grew beside it takes another step. I would give students historic and modern maps of the same Texas area, along with a short account of transportation, water access, and climate.

Set up and model, eight minutes: Demonstrate one settlement claim using a map feature and a detail from the account. Explain how an observation differs from a possible cause. A map can show where settlements appeared, but establishing why they grew requires additional evidence.

Investigate, ten minutes: Have partners mark settlements on both maps and identify a pattern. Their explanation should connect two map details to water, terrain, or climate while considering another influence, such as transportation or employment.

For example, students might compare settlements near a mountain pass with those on open plains. Ask: “Which evidence would make you revise your explanation?”

This work addresses the original lesson’s focus on "Texas TEKS §113.15(c)(7)(A–B)" (https://tea.texas.gov/laws-and-rules/sboe-rules-tac/sboe-tac-currently-effect/ch113a.pdf): geographic influences on settlement and settlement patterns at different times.

2. Creating and exchanging questions makes students examine their reasoning

Build and test, fifteen minutes: Open "SplatWorks QuizSplat" (https://splatworks.org/solutions/quiz-flashcard-studio/) and replace the sample items with three questions about the lesson:

- A straightforward check of understanding
- A question that exposes a misconception
- A new example that requires students to apply their explanation

Before entering the questions, students draft an answer key on paper and explain why each answer makes sense.

Have another pair try the set, either on screen or with printable flashcards. Ask players to explain the evidence behind their answers. The authors then record one correction and save or export their questions.

A partner’s response gives students something specific to review: whether their question is clear and whether their answer follows from the evidence.

3. An individual response shows what each student can apply

Check learning, seven minutes: Give each student a third small map. Ask them to suggest a plausible settlement location, explain one geographic advantage, and identify an uncertainty.

Collect responses independently so you can see each student’s understanding after the partner activity. Look for accurate content, relevant evidence, and a reasonable connection between them. Use an incomplete explanation to choose a follow-up question or brief reteaching example.

Offer vocabulary cards, an explanation stem, or oral rehearsal as needed. With one device, project QuizSplat and have students work on paper. Each option should preserve the same expectation: explain a settlement choice using evidence.
