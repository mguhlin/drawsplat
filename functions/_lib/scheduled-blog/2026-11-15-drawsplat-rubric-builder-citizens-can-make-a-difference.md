Help students improve a proposal for preserving a fictional oral-history collection by checking whether the action, evidence, and community benefit are clear.

## 1. Preserving a collection gives voluntary service a concrete purpose

Imagine a local collection of recorded memories that needs organizing. "Let's save it" sounds good. What would the volunteers actually do, and whose permission would they need?

Use eight minutes to model a respectful request with a problem, evidence, and a feasible action. Provide a fictional collection description and the roles of the people responsible for it. Give partners ten minutes to plan an appropriate contribution. They should identify who can authorize access or use and how the work would help the community. Keep the request in class; this lesson doesn't require anyone to contact a real organization.

*For planning: Grade 4 Social Studies, 40 minutes; [Texas TEKS §113.15(c)(15)(B)](https://tea.texas.gov/laws-and-rules/sboe-rules-tac/sboe-tac-currently-effect/ch113a.pdf). [TCEA Essential Learning Expectations](https://mglearn.github.io/eles): S3.2 and S4.2.*

## 2. Clear criteria make feedback more useful than a score

Allow fifteen minutes with [SplatWorks RubricSplat](https://splatworks.org/solutions/rubric-builder/). Adapt a template around three checks: accurate content, relevant evidence, and a clear explanation. Describe what a reader could actually notice. "Names the person whose permission is needed" is much easier to act on than "excellent plan."

Have students try the rubric on their proposal, then ask a partner to identify one improvement. Revise and keep both versions. Print or copy the rubric for students working on paper. Offer vocabulary cards and a chance to say the request aloud before drafting it.

## 3. A new civic action reveals whether the criteria made sense

In seven minutes, ask each student to suggest a different voluntary action for a new fictional community problem. Who could help? Which detail supports the plan?

Look for a feasible contribution and an explanation of its benefit. If the student can name a criterion but cannot apply it, walk through an example together before adding more rubric rows.
