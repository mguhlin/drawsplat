Let students build and check a lunch-choice graph, explaining how its scale turns drawn bars into quantities.

## 1. A frequency table gives every bar something to answer to

For this lesson, lunch choices are fictional: 12, 8, 6, and 10. You can name the four categories without asking anyone about their actual lunch.

Use eight minutes to model a table and a labeled bar graph with intervals of two. Partners spend ten minutes checking each bar against its frequency, finding a difference, and combining two categories. Ask how the display would look with intervals of one. The bars may occupy a different number of intervals, but twelve still means twelve.

*For planning: Grade 3 Math, 40 minutes; [Texas TEKS §111.5(b)(8)(A–B)](https://tea.texas.gov/laws-and-rules/sboe-rules-tac/sboe-tac-currently-effect/ch111a.pdf). [TCEA Essential Learning Expectations](https://mglearn.github.io/eles): S3.2 and S4.2.*

## 2. Checking the digital graph keeps its appearance from settling the question

Allow fifteen minutes in [SplatWorks GraphSplat](https://splatworks.org/solutions/graphsplat/). Choose a bar-chart workspace, enter the practice table, and label the chart and axes. Read the displayed scale before comparing it with your intervals-of-two model.

Have a partner verify each bar against the original table. If something doesn't match, choose that as the correction to make and explain what changed. Export with the available download option, keeping the table beside the graph. Students can talk through one difference and one combined total before writing; a projected graph and paper responses work with one device.

## 3. A different scale checks whether students can read beyond their own graph

Save seven minutes for each student to read a new scaled graph, answer two questions, and explain the values used.

I'd pay attention to a well-executed calculation with the wrong starting numbers. That student may need help reading the axis, not another round of subtraction. The explanation tells you where to begin.
