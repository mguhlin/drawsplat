Give students a way to check their missing-side perimeter solutions by looking for a complete boundary, consistent units, and an equation that works.

## 1. A missing side makes the total boundary useful

We know the perimeter is 22 units. Three sides measure 5, 4, and 6. What's left? Students can use that question to explain why the unknown side must be 7 units.

Spend eight minutes tracing a garden-plan boundary on grid paper and labeling every side. Distinguish linear units around the edge from square units inside. Give partners ten minutes to solve the missing-side example and verify it by adding all the lengths. A six-by-four rectangle, with perimeter 20 units, offers a second model to compare.

*For planning: Grade 3 Math, 40 minutes; [Texas TEKS §111.5(b)(7)(B)](https://tea.texas.gov/laws-and-rules/sboe-rules-tac/sboe-tac-currently-effect/ch111a.pdf). [TCEA Essential Learning Expectations](https://mglearn.github.io/eles): S3.2 and S4.2.*

## 2. Observable criteria give students something they can actually fix

Set aside fifteen minutes in [SplatWorks RubricSplat](https://splatworks.org/solutions/rubric-builder/). Adapt a template around accurate content, relevant evidence, and a clear explanation. Make the descriptions specific: every side is labeled, the units match, and the equation checks the perimeter.

Have students assess their solution and ask a partner to identify one criterion needing work. Revise, then keep the first and improved responses with a printed or copied rubric. A student who struggles to write can explain the checking equation aloud first. Vocabulary cards and a paper version keep the task accessible with one projected device.

## 3. A new boundary checks whether students can apply the criteria alone

Use seven minutes for a fresh missing-side problem. Each student supplies a labeled boundary and an equation that verifies the answer.

When the numbers don't add up, the rubric should help the student locate the trouble. If it only produces a score, make the descriptions more useful together. "Check every side" is a direction a learner can act on.
