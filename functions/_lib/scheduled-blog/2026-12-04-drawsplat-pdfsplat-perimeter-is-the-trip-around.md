Put two different rectangles side by side so students can explain how a six-by-four garden and a seven-by-three garden both have a perimeter of twenty units.

## 1. Different shapes make the shared perimeter worth checking

They don't look quite alike. Can the trip around each still be the same length? Let's trace it before deciding.

Use eight minutes to model boundary labels on a garden drawing with grid paper. Distinguish the linear units around the edge from the square units covering the inside. Partners get ten minutes to check 6 + 4 + 6 + 4 and 7 + 3 + 7 + 3. Both total 20 units. Include a missing-side example: perimeter 22 with known sides 5, 4, and 6 leaves 7 units to account for.

*For planning: Grade 3 Math, 40 minutes; [Texas TEKS §111.5(b)(7)(B)](https://tea.texas.gov/laws-and-rules/sboe-rules-tac/sboe-tac-currently-effect/ch111a.pdf). [TCEA Essential Learning Expectations](https://mglearn.github.io/eles): S3.2 and S4.2.*

## 2. Annotations let students show which lengths they counted

Give pairs fifteen minutes in [SplatWorks PDFSplat](https://splatworks.org/solutions/pdfsplat/) with a teacher-provided PDF of the two rectangles. Trace the boundary, label sides, and add an equation and a short explanation.

Ask a partner to locate each term of the equation on the drawing. Choose one missing or unclear annotation to fix, then export the annotated PDF and retain the original. A page covered in highlighting won't make the route easier to follow. Students can rehearse their explanation aloud or use printed copies and vocabulary cards while one device projects the example.

## 3. A new missing side checks whether the boundary idea is secure

For seven minutes, each student solves another missing-side problem and supplies a labeled boundary with a checking equation.

If the answer relies on counting interior squares, go back to the trip around the outside. The drawing can help students see exactly which question they have answered.
