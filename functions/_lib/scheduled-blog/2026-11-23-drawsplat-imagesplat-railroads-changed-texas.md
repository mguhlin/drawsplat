Have students annotate a railroad map to explain a change in cotton transport, distinguishing what the map shows from what a written account supports.

## 1. A route and a written account answer different questions

A line on a map can show where the railroad ran. Can it tell us what happened to a cotton producer's business? We'll need another source for that part.

Gather two dated Texas rail maps and short, reliable accounts of a town and its cotton trade. Use eight minutes to model a transportation claim with a map observation and an account detail. Partners spend ten minutes tracing a route, locating a town, and explaining a supported change in access to markets. Ask how the effect on a producer might differ from the effect on residents. Avoid assuming every rail town grew.

*For planning: Grade 4 Social Studies, 40 minutes; [Texas TEKS §113.15(c)(4)(C)](https://tea.texas.gov/laws-and-rules/sboe-rules-tac/sboe-tac-currently-effect/ch113a.pdf). [TCEA Essential Learning Expectations](https://mglearn.github.io/eles): S3.2 and S4.2.*

## 2. Annotations keep the claim beside the evidence that supports it

Allow fifteen minutes with [SplatWorks ImageSplat](https://splatworks.org/solutions/imagesplat/). Import a teacher-provided map image, keeping the original on its own layer. Mark the route and town, then add a short note connecting a relevant account detail to the transport claim.

Have a partner ask, "Which source tells us that?" Choose one overextended claim or unclear arrow to fix. Export the PNG and retain the source separately. The same work can begin on a printed map, with oral rehearsal and vocabulary cards to help students get their explanation started.

## 3. Another town shows whether the source habit carries over

For seven minutes, give each student a brief account of a different community. Ask for one railroad effect and the detail supporting it.

I'd be pleased to hear a student say a source doesn't establish a proposed benefit. Knowing when to stop a claim is part of using evidence, too.
