Have students design a twenty-unit rectangular boundary so they can explain how every side contributes to the perimeter.

## 1. Designing a boundary gives the total a job to do

Twenty units of edging, and a rectangular garden to plan. What side lengths could work? Students have something to decide before they draw the first line.

Spend eight minutes modeling a labeled rectangle on grid paper and tracing its boundary. Explain linear units around the edge and square units inside. Give partners ten minutes to check a six-by-four rectangle and propose another rectangle with perimeter 20. Include a missing-side example with perimeter 22 and known sides 5, 4, and 6. Have them verify the remaining 7 by addition.

*For planning: Grade 3 Math, 40 minutes; [Texas TEKS §111.5(b)(7)(B)](https://tea.texas.gov/laws-and-rules/sboe-rules-tac/sboe-tac-currently-effect/ch111a.pdf). [TCEA Essential Learning Expectations](https://mglearn.github.io/eles): S3.2 and S4.2.*

## 2. A diagram makes the checking sequence visible

Allow fifteen minutes with [SplatWorks DiagramSplat](https://splatworks.org/solutions/mermaid/) and a teacher-provided flowchart scaffold. Show the chosen side lengths, the full boundary sum, and the check against 20. Keep labels short and watch the live preview after changes.

Ask a partner to follow the arrows and explain why the design works. Choose one unclear label to fix, then export PNG or SVG. A scaffold means students can spend their time on the mathematics rather than untangling syntax. Paper diagrams, vocabulary cards, and oral rehearsal offer another way to do the same thinking.

## 3. A fresh missing side checks whether the idea extends beyond the design

For seven minutes, give each student a new missing-side perimeter problem. Collect a labeled boundary and an equation that checks the result.

If the drawing and equation disagree, trace each side together. That's a useful place for feedback: the student can see which length hasn't yet found its way into the calculation.
