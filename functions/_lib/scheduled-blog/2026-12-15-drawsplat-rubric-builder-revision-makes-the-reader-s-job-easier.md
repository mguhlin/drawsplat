Give students clear revision criteria so they can combine repeated ideas into a sentence that preserves the message and helps the reader.

## 1. Repetition gives writers a concrete problem to solve

Two sentences, much the same idea. Do we need both? Sometimes the useful part is tucked into the repetition, so deleting a whole sentence may lose something worth keeping.

Prepare a short teacher-written paragraph with a repeated idea, a confusing sequence, and a missing explanation. Use eight minutes to model one substantive revision and explain its benefit. Give partners ten minutes to propose two changes and read the result aloud. Focus on combining the repeated material while preserving important information. Adding adjectives or fixing punctuation won't answer this particular writing question.

*For planning: Grade 4 ELA/Reading, 40 minutes; [Texas TEKS §110.6(b)(11)(C)](https://tea.texas.gov/laws-and-rules/sboe-rules-tac/sboe-tac-currently-effect/ch110a.pdf). [TCEA Essential Learning Expectations](https://mglearn.github.io/eles): S3.2 and S4.2.*

## 2. Specific criteria make feedback usable

Allow fifteen minutes in [SplatWorks RubricSplat](https://splatworks.org/solutions/rubric-builder/) to adapt a template around accurate content, relevant evidence, and a clear explanation. For this response, describe whether the combined sentence keeps the important information and removes unnecessary duplication.

Students try the rubric, then ask a partner to name one improvement. Revise and retain both versions, along with a printed or copied rubric. Let students say the combined sentence aloud before writing it. A sentence stem or vocabulary card can help them explain the change, and paper copies work with one projected device.

## 3. A new paragraph shows whether the criteria guide another revision

For seven minutes, each student repairs one structural problem in a fresh paragraph and explains how the change helps its reader.

Ask what was kept and what was removed. If the new sentence is shorter but loses the message, read the versions together. Brevity helps when the reader still gets the idea.
