A simple sketch of a fictional dam project can help students compare flood-management benefits with changes to river flow and identify evidence they still need.

## 1. A shared case keeps the comparison connected to consequences

"The dam will help." Help with what, and for whom? Those are useful questions to put beside a map.

Prepare a fictional Texas reservoir case with a before-and-after map, a water-supply need, and three stakeholder statements. Label the scenario as fictional. Spend eight minutes modeling a supported benefit and cost. Give partners ten minutes to compare flood management with changes in river flow, identifying who or what experiences each consequence. Ask what additional evidence would help make the comparison fair. Students can reach different recommendations if they explain them from the case.

*For planning: Grade 4 Social Studies, 40 minutes; [Texas TEKS §113.15(c)(8)(A–C)](https://tea.texas.gov/laws-and-rules/sboe-rules-tac/sboe-tac-currently-effect/ch113a.pdf). [TCEA Essential Learning Expectations](https://mglearn.github.io/eles): S3.2 and S4.2.*

## 2. A sketch gives partners a way to question the relationship

Allow fifteen minutes with [SplatWorks Draw & Sketch](https://splatworks.org/solutions/drawsketch/) to draw the central relationship. Put labels in the freehand drawing or on a separate sheet. Keep it simple enough that a partner can explain the intended change before the artist speaks.

Choose one confusing mark or label to improve, then save PNG and collect a written or oral explanation alongside it. Drawing skill shouldn't decide whose reasoning gets heard. Offer vocabulary cards and rehearsal time; a paper sketch works just as well when one device has to serve the group.

## 3. A road case checks whether students can compare another environmental change

For seven minutes, each student identifies the purpose, one benefit, and one cost of road clearing in a new case, supporting the response with details.

I'd also welcome a question about missing information. Students are learning to compare consequences, and sometimes the most useful next move is knowing what else to ask.
