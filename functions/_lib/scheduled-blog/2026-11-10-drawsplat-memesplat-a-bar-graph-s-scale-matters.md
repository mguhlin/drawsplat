Ask students to explain a bar graph's scale in a caption that makes sense only when they've read the numbers correctly.

## 1. The scale gives each drawn interval its value

Four intervals tall means four, right? Well, that depends. This is a good place to slow down before anyone starts subtracting.

Use fictional travel totals: walking 8, bus 12, car 6, bicycle 4. Spend eight minutes modeling a frequency table and a bar graph with intervals of two. Label the title, categories, and axes. Give partners ten minutes to check every bar, find how many more chose bus than bicycle, and combine walking with bicycle. Ask what changes if the intervals become ones. The display changes; those practice totals stay put.

*For planning: Grade 3 Math, 40 minutes; [Texas TEKS §111.5(b)(8)(A–B)](https://tea.texas.gov/laws-and-rules/sboe-rules-tac/sboe-tac-currently-effect/ch111a.pdf). [TCEA Essential Learning Expectations](https://mglearn.github.io/eles): S3.2 and S4.2.*

## 2. A short caption makes the scale hard to ignore

Give pairs fifteen minutes in [SplatWorks MemeSplat](https://splatworks.org/solutions/memesplat/) to create a visual about reading the scale. A classroom template or local image will do. Add a caption and two sentences explaining the calculation behind it.

Could someone who missed the lesson misunderstand the image? Have a partner check that before the pair chooses one change and downloads the PNG. A joke is welcome, but the scale still has to be right. Students who need it can talk through the explanation first or work with a printed graph.

## 3. A new graph separates scale reading from remembering the example

For seven minutes, let each student tackle a different scaled graph, one difference question, and one combined total. Ask them to explain where the values came from.

A correct subtraction built from the wrong bar values is useful information. Go back to the axis together. More arithmetic practice won't fix a scale that the reader hasn't noticed.
