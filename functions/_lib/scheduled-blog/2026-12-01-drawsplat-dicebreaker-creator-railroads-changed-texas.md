A partner discussion can help students separate a source-supported railroad effect from a benefit they are only predicting.

## 1. A town gaining rail access gives students a claim to investigate

The train is coming to town. Good news for everyone? That's a question for the sources, and it gives students a reason to look closely.

Gather two dated Texas rail maps and short, reliable accounts of a community and an industry. Spend eight minutes modeling a map observation and a supported economic effect. Partners get ten minutes to trace the connection and compare what it could mean for producers and residents. Ask them to identify one change the account establishes and one benefit they would need more evidence to claim. Not every town shared the same story.

*For planning: Grade 4 Social Studies, 40 minutes; [Texas TEKS §113.15(c)(4)(C)](https://tea.texas.gov/laws-and-rules/sboe-rules-tac/sboe-tac-currently-effect/ch113a.pdf). [TCEA Essential Learning Expectations](https://mglearn.github.io/eles): S3.2 and S4.2.*

## 2. Required evidence turns give every partner a chance to explain

Give students fifteen minutes with [SplatWorks Dicebreaker Creator](https://splatworks.org/solutions/dicebreakers/). Put prompts on six faces: name an idea, identify evidence, explain a relationship, find a misconception, change an example, and ask a question. Export a grid or strip, or share the student view.

Both partners answer the evidence and explanation prompts regardless of the roll. Record those answers on paper. Have each choose one prediction to qualify or support more clearly after the discussion. Oral rehearsal and a short vocabulary card can help; the printed version works without separate devices.

## 3. Another community checks whether students return to the evidence

For seven minutes, each student explains one railroad effect in a new community account and identifies the supporting detail.

If the response begins with what "must have happened," ask where the source says so. That's a worthwhile habit for a young historian to practice, even when the prediction sounds quite reasonable.
