A simple revision diagram can help students explain how deleting an unrelated sentence and improving a transition make a paragraph easier to follow.

## 1. An interruption gives coherence a problem students can hear

A paragraph is moving along, and suddenly there's a sentence about something else. Read it aloud. Where did the reader lose the thread?

Prepare a short teacher-written draft with a confusing sequence, repetition, and a missing explanation. Spend eight minutes modeling one change and naming its benefit. Give partners ten minutes to propose two substantive revisions. For this version, delete an unrelated sentence and improve a transition. Read the result aloud again, keeping the intended message. A spelling fix or decorative adjective won't repair that interruption.

*For planning: Grade 4 ELA/Reading, 40 minutes; [Texas TEKS §110.6(b)(11)(C)](https://tea.texas.gov/laws-and-rules/sboe-rules-tac/sboe-tac-currently-effect/ch110a.pdf). [TCEA Essential Learning Expectations](https://mglearn.github.io/eles): S3.2 and S4.2.*

## 2. A diagram makes the order of ideas available for discussion

Set aside fifteen minutes with [SplatWorks DiagramSplat](https://splatworks.org/solutions/mermaid/) and a teacher-provided flowchart scaffold. Replace sample labels with the paragraph's main ideas. Show the original sequence and the improved connection, checking the live preview as you change labels.

Ask a partner to follow the arrows and explain what became easier to read. Choose one unclear label to revise, then export PNG or SVG. Students can rehearse aloud and use a sentence stem for the explanation. Paper diagrams and a projected example work when devices are scarce; there's no need to make syntax the obstacle.

## 3. Another paragraph checks whether students can improve coherence alone

Use the last seven minutes for each student to repair a structural problem in a new paragraph and explain how the change helps its reader.

Ask what the transition now connects. If the answer is only "I added then," return to the two ideas on either side. The relationship between them is what the reader needs.
